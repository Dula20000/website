# Taiwan Trip

[As we were landing, I noticed (though I only understood this later) that the airport was a bit far from the main city, Taipei, and closer to a rural area. So I expected the whole country to look like paddy fields and rice fields, and I landed with that expectation.(video)]

[Once we landed, I met up with all my mates from my cohort, and we went through customs and security. The professor was waiting right outside, and we went with him to a restaurant in the airport. Then there was a bus to take us into the city. As we got closer and closer, buildings started to appear, and the transition happened gradually. It took about 40 minutes, and within that time everything changed. The city wasn't what I'd pictured, but I was fine with either outcome. I'd been expecting Taiwan to be essentially a "China lite" version, and I knew beforehand that China has both rural tier 3 to 4 cities and major tier 1 cities like Shenzhen and Chongqing, so I was prepared for any mix of that. It was really just a matter of managing my expectations, I wouldn't have minded either way.]

[The first thing we noticed right out of the gate was the humidity. Honestly, looking back, the first few days weren't much fun. It was too humid, and while the temperature itself was about average, the humidity made it miserable. I'd sweat profusely just from a bit of light cardio or even just walking.(1 photo)]

[On the first day we went to check out the university, the alma mater of our professor, The {National Taiwan University}. He was genuinely upbeat about it, since it had been about 35 years since he'd last been back. ][It was probably more exciting for him than for us, but we enjoyed it too. It's one of the top-ranked universities in the country, and one of its specialties is electrical engineering, so we visited those buildings.]

[The next day, we also visited one of the old traditional temples. Interestingly, temples in Taiwan tend to blend three religious traditions: some Buddhist elements, some Taoist elements, and a third one I can't quite recall.]

[Whenever I had free time, I went to a bouldering place to do some rock climbing, and there was also a nice driving range we visited. Most of the time, though, we stayed together as a group, we went almost everywhere together, which was actually a nice thing.(2 photos)]

## Taipei 101 and the Google Office

[A few days in, we hit one of the highlights of the trip: a visit to {Taipei 101}, including exclusive access to the Google office located very high up in the building. From there I could see Taipei's airport. An interesting fact about that office: the American rock climber {Alex Honnold} made history as the first person to free-solo Taipei 101, climbing it without ropes or any safety equipment (if he'd slipped, that would have been it). He climbed past the Google office during the ascent, and the office posted footage of it from their own perspective online, which made the office semi-famous within the building. We got to visit that same office and sat in on seminars about AI and its relevance to nanotechnology, as well as {TPUs} (Tensor Processing Units), which is the direction Google is betting on instead of Nvidia's GPUs.(2 photos)]

## Elephant Mountain and Lungshan Temple

[After that we went to {Elephant Mountain}, which has a great viewpoint over Taipei 101.][ Some things happened while I was flying my drone there, and without going into detail, I ended up losing it. (video)]I might post the footage at some point.

[We also visited {Lungshan Temple (龍山寺)}, located in an area historically associated with underrepresented and vulnerable people, who would go there for refuge. At the temple there's a tradition involving two half-moon-shaped wooden blocks that you drop on the ground. The result functions almost like a binary read: two "smiley" (convex) faces or two "sad" (flat) faces are not the outcome you want, but one of each means you're in luck. I dropped mine and got the lucky result. It's not scientific, obviously, just a bit of fun local tradition. One guy in our group was unlucky the entire trip. He kept trying to get the lucky combination so he could receive a red string, but every single time he got double smiley faces instead. It became a running joke.(3 photos)]

## Infrastructure

[I loved the infrastructure: well thought out, robust, and genuinely deep. It wasn't just the metro system; there was supporting infrastructure around it too, like rentable bikes right outside metro stations. Good, easy mobility like that tends to make an economy move faster and more efficiently, so it's a great sign for a country. The metro frequency was also excellent, about every four minutes, so even if you missed one, the wait wasn't bad.(1 video or photo and 1 definite photo)]

## National Tsing Hua University and the Nuclear Reactor

[We visited National Tsing Hua University in Taiwan, not to be confused with China's top-tier Tsinghua University. There's some history there around the two institutions and how the Taiwanese one has, at times, positioned itself as connected to a broader "China" identity, though I don't know the full details. At this campus we visited a nuclear reactor facility, which was one of the leading centers for boron neutron capture therapy, used to treat certain medical conditions. We got to go inside the reactor building and see the pool that housed the nuclear rods.(3 photos)]

## Central Taiwan

[We then traveled to central Taiwan, which had a lot to see. It used to be a resource-extraction hub, so there's old rail infrastructure still around. We visited Sun Moon Lake, which was beautiful, and took a boat ride on it. We also visited a well-known Taoist temple nearby. The food throughout was consistently great. We also visited a paper factory and made our own paper by hand. (4 photos)]

[The academics leading up to this point had been intense. We were cramming roughly four months' worth of material into a single month, which was never going to be fun, but it was the trade-off for being on the study abroad, and honestly a fair one. If you didn't like that arrangement, there wasn't much room to complain; that was simply the deal.(just text slide)]

## TSMC

[One of the highlights was visiting TSMC. We toured the museum and learned about the history of nanotechnology and chip manufacturing, and how Taiwan became the dominant player in the chip industry. Part of the strategy was staying a neutral manufacturing party rather than producing devices that competed with its own customers, unlike, say, Samsung, which both manufactured chips and was accused of copying designs from companies like Apple, creating a trust issue. TSMC's founder reportedly hit a "glass ceiling" in the US industry and returned to Taiwan to start the company instead. It was a great visit overall.(2 photos)]

## Tea Village and Taiwan's Tea Culture

[As the academic side of the program wound down, we had more free time. We took a roughly 20-minute cable car ride to a remote tea village, where we drank tea, talked, and took in the view from a distance. The tea was excellent. In my opinion, the best tea in the world is {Ceylon tea}, Sri Lankan tea, and Taiwanese tea comes right after it (I may be a little biased here 😁). As a shameless plug: if you're looking for Ceylon tea, {Dilmah} is a well-known Sri Lankan brand, reportedly a favorite of {Margot Robbie's}, for what that's worth.(just text slide)]

[More broadly, the food and drink profile of the country is very tea-heavy. The most prevalent variety by far is winter melon tea, which is a bit sweet and takes some getting used to, but grows on you. What struck me is just how much variety there is around it. In a lot of countries, say Turkey, there's essentially one default tea and that's what you get; there isn't much room for variation. In Taiwan it's the opposite: there are many different types and flavors of tea in circulation. Winter melon tea has the highest prevalence of any of them, but it's not overwhelmingly dominant, there are plenty of other tea varieties that are also decently common.(2 photos)]

[On the protein side, tofu, pork, beef, and chicken are all common, though chicken specifically was hardest to find. Protein Box (more on that below) was really the only reliable source I found for it. There's also stinky tofu, which lives up to its name, the smell is strong if you're near it. It's a bit like durian: an acquired taste that I personally didn't enjoy, but plenty of people love it.(1 photo)]

## National Taiwan University Semiconductor Lab

[One more place worth mentioning: before the tea village, we visited a semiconductor fabrication lab at National Taiwan University, a research center tied to a consortium focused on key technologies, working on electromechanical systems (NEMS-related research). There, we etched our own wafers using a custom design we made ourselves. It was a simplified process, obviously nothing close to TSMC's precision, but genuinely interesting to go through.(2 photos)]

## Transportation System

[Worth going into more detail here. The metro system is the backbone of the whole city, a wide network that doesn't necessarily go extremely deep in any one area, but reaches broadly enough to touch all the main points of the city. Buses form a second layer, running from each station out to wherever you need to go. The U-Bike rental system is arguably a third layer (or second, depending on your preference for biking versus taking the bus), you can easily rent a bike and ride to your destination.

Between these layers, getting from point A to point B anywhere in the city is easy; you don't have to think much about it. All you need is an {EasyCard}, which is loosely tied to your identity via your phone number but isn't a hard form of ID, you can hold multiple EasyCards. You can top up your balance at most convenience stores (Family Mart, 7-Eleven) and at metro stations, then use it to pay for both bikes and the metro. We had unlimited passes for our trip, so it didn't cost us anything each time we scanned, but for a regular user that's how the system works.

[{U-Bikes} are free for the first 30 minutes, and given how the transportation network is laid out, you're unlikely to need a bike for anywhere near that long, most rides end up being 10 to 15 minutes at most.

On top of these three layers within the city, there's also high-speed rail connecting different cities. There's no need for high-speed rail within a single city, since Taiwan's cities (and the country overall) are compact enough that it wouldn't make financial or practical sense, that would just be spending money for no real benefit.(just text slide)]

The metro system itself is top-notch. There's also a well-known jingle that plays when a train arrives, at least on the green line. I'll add the name of it later.(1 video or photo 1 definite photo)]
