Propulsion Research Series · 2026

Four propulsion tools, built from scratch and pointed at real engines

Dulran · Aerospace Engineering, UT Austin · September 2026

Each study follows the same pattern. I wrote the solver myself, verified it against exact or closed-form solutions (with observed orders of accuracy, conservation checks and grid studies), and then ran it on representative inputs from public sources: Merlin, RS-25 and Raptor-class engines, KNSB and APCP motors, and CFM56, LEAP and GE90-class turbofans. Every page includes a JavaScript port of the solver that runs in your browser and is cross-checked against the Python reference to round-off.

No. 1 · Compressible flow / CFD

Quasi-1D Euler CFD of rocket nozzles

HLLC finite-volume solver with MUSCL reconstruction and SSP-RK3, verified against the exact nozzle-shock solution. It sweeps three engine classes through the 1976 US Standard Atmosphere to map over-expansion and separation risk.

An RS-25-class nozzle (ε = 69) exits at about 0.21 × sea-level ambient pressure, past the Summerfield separation line. Only its 20.6 MPa chamber pressure makes that area ratio viable at all.

No. 2 · Thermochemistry

Chemical equilibrium for liquid bipropellants

Gibbs minimisation in the style of NASA RP-1311 over 17 C–H–O species, with frozen and shifting nozzle expansion. Applied to kerolox, methalox and hydrolox engines.

Hydrolox Isp peaks near O/F 4.8, yet RS-25 runs at 6: it gives up about 3 s of Isp for about 14 % denser propellant.

No. 3 · Solid propulsion

SRM internal ballistics with level-set grain regression

Fast-marching burn-back of arbitrary grain cross-sections coupled to lumped chamber ballistics, with a 20,000-sample Monte Carlo on propellant and throat tolerances.

The 3σ-equivalent MEOP comes out 1.26× the nominal peak pressure. Burn-rate exponent n looks dominant only because tables quote a at a 1 MPa reference.

No. 4 · Air-breathing propulsion

Why turbofans keep getting bigger

Mattingly-style on- and off-design cycle analysis with variable-property gas and turbine cooling air. Includes a carpet-plot trade over bypass ratio, fan pressure ratio and turbine inlet temperature.

A LEAP-1A-class engine burns 14.4 % less fuel per unit thrust than a CFM56-class one, about two-thirds of it from propulsive efficiency. With nacelle drag included, the optimum bypass ratio is about 14.

§How to read these

Inputs are representative values from public sources (textbooks, NASA reports, manufacturer figures). Treat them as approximate; each page gives its sources and assumptions in an inputs table. Where a model disagrees with a published figure, the page says so rather than retuning to match. Every number on each page is loaded from the JSON that the study's run_study.py generates, so the results can be reproduced from the code in the repository.
