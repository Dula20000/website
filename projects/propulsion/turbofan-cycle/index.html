Why turbofans keep getting bigger: a parametric and off-design cycle study of bypass ratio, fan pressure ratio and turbine inlet temperature

Dulran · September 2026

variable cp (NASA polynomials) turbine cooling bleed two-spool, separate exhaust off-design matching 1976 US Standard Atmosphere Python + JS port

Abstract

I wrote an on-design and off-design cycle code for a two-spool, separate-exhaust turbofan, following Mattingly's parametric and performance analysis, then used it to ask why bypass ratio keeps rising and what stops it. The gas model is thermally perfect: air and Jet-A combustion products take their properties from NASA 7-coefficient polynomials, and the fuel-air ratio comes from an enthalpy balance on a common reference state. A simple turbine cooling-air bleed ( of core flow, assumed) is included. In the constant-cp, loss-free limit the code matches the closed-form ideal turbofan to relative error. Energy and spool power balances close to round-off. The numerically found optimum fan pressure ratio agrees with the analytic ideal-cycle optimum and with an exact first-order condition I derive for the lossy cycle. On representative public inputs, a CFM56-7B-class engine at M 0.78 / 35,000 ft gives a cruise TSFC of lb/(lbf·h) ( for Tt4 ± K). That is somewhat below the ≈0.63–0.67 region quoted publicly; only the top of the band reaches it. A LEAP-1A-class cycle comes out lower, against the manufacturer's ≈15 % claim. About of that gain comes from propulsive efficiency. On its own, the cycle keeps improving up to a bypass ratio of ≈. Nacelle drag and engine weight both scale with fan area, and adding them pulls the optimum back to ≈ for my baseline installation assumptions (range when those penalties are halved or doubled). The installed optimum moves up by roughly in bypass ratio per 100 K of turbine inlet temperature. That is the short answer to the title: hotter cores keep pushing the optimum fan larger.

1Motivation

Civil turbofan bypass ratio has gone from about 1 on the first turbofans, to about 5 on the CFM56 family, to about 11 on the LEAP and PW1000G. Fan diameter has grown with it, until it now sets landing-gear length and nacelle drag. The textbook reason is propulsive efficiency: moving a lot of air slowly takes less energy per unit thrust than moving a little air fast. That argument has no stopping point, though, and real engines do stop. So I wanted to answer three questions with a tool I could check line by line:

  1. How much of the improvement from a CFM56-class to a LEAP-class engine is propulsive, and how much is thermal?
  2. For a given core (OPR, Tt4, component efficiencies), where is the best fan pressure ratio and bypass ratio, both uninstalled and once nacelle drag and weight are charged to the engine?
  3. How does a fixed design behave away from its design point (thrust lapse with altitude and Mach, TSFC at part throttle)?

2Method

2.1 Engine model and station numbering

The engine is a two-spool, separate-exhaust turbofan with stations numbered as in Mattingly [1]. The low-pressure (LP) spool's turbine drives the fan, which compresses the bypass stream by \(\pi_f\), and the LPC, which compresses the core stream by \(\pi_{cL}\) (fan root plus booster). The high-pressure (HP) spool's turbine drives the HPC (\(\pi_{cH}\)). The overall pressure ratio is \(\mathrm{OPR}=\pi_{cL}\pi_{cH}\). A turbojet is the same model with \(\alpha=0\).

2.2 On-design (parametric) analysis

The cycle bookkeeping follows Mattingly's parametric analysis [1]: polytropic efficiencies for fan, LPC, HPC, HPT and LPT; total-pressure ratios for inlet (MIL-E-5007 ram recovery above Mach 1), burner and nozzles; burner efficiency \(\eta_b\); one mechanical efficiency per spool. The thermodynamics is thermally perfect rather than Mattingly's two constant-cp gases. Each gas is an ideal-gas mixture whose \(c_p(T)\), sensible enthalpy \(h(T)\) and entropy function \(s^\circ(T)\) come from NASA 7-coefficient polynomials (GRI-Mech 3.0 data for N2, O2, Ar, CO2, H2O [8, 9]). Combustion products follow from complete lean combustion of a CH1.92 Jet-A surrogate in dry air, so their properties depend on the fuel-air ratio. All enthalpies are sensible enthalpies measured from 298.15 K, the reference state of the lower heating value \(h_{PR}=42.8\) MJ/kg, and the fuel enters at 298.15 K. The burner fuel-air ratio \(f_b\) (per kg of burner air) then solves

$$h_{air}(T_{t3}) + f_b\,\eta_b h_{PR} = (1+f_b)\,h_{prod}(T_{t4}, f_b).$$

Along any process of fixed composition \(s^\circ(T_2)-s^\circ(T_1)=R\ln(P_2/P_1)\) for an isentrope, so polytropic compressors and turbines are

$$s^\circ(T_{t,out})-s^\circ(T_{t,in}) = \frac{R}{e_c}\ln\pi_c,\qquad s^\circ(T_{t,out})-s^\circ(T_{t,in}) = e_t R\ln\pi_t,$$

and the spool power balances use enthalpy differences:

$$\eta_{mH}(1-\varepsilon)(1+f_b)\,[h_4-h_{4.4}] = h_3-h_{2.5},\qquad \eta_{mL}(1+f)\,[h_{4.5}-h_5] = (h_{2.5}-h_2) + \alpha(h_{13}-h_2).$$

Cooling air. A fraction \(\varepsilon\) of the core air is bled after the HPC. It bypasses the burner and HPT and is remixed at constant total pressure ahead of the LPT: \((1-\varepsilon)(1+f_b)h_{prod}(T_{t4.4},f_b)+\varepsilon h_{air}(T_{t3})=(1+f)h_{prod}(T_{t4.5},f)\), with \(f=(1-\varepsilon)f_b\) the fuel per unit core air (the mixture of products and air is again "products" at the overall fuel-air ratio). Coolant that does no HPT work is a conservative simplification. I use \(\varepsilon=\) for every engine as an assumption. Fallback. With thermo='cpg', \(h=c_pT\) and \(s^\circ=c_p\ln T\), so the same code reduces to Mattingly's two-gas "modified" cycle and, with one gas and all losses off, to the ideal cycle (§3.1).

Nozzles expand isentropically from \((T_{t9},P_{t9})\): \(s^\circ(T_9)=s^\circ(T_{t9})+R\ln(P_9/P_{t9})\), \(V_9=\sqrt{2[h(T_{t9})-h(T_9)]}\). A convergent nozzle chokes when the static pressure at \(M=1\) (from \(2[h(T_t)-h(T^*)]=\gamma(T^*)RT^*\)) exceeds \(P_0\), and then produces pressure thrust. The "expanded" option sets \(P_9=P_0\). Thrust per unit total inlet air, TSFC (with \(f\) per unit core air) and the efficiencies are:

$$\frac{F}{\dot m_0} = \frac{1}{1+\alpha}\Big\{(1+f)V_9 - V_0 + (1+f)\frac{R_tT_9}{V_9}\Big(1-\frac{P_0}{P_9}\Big) + \alpha\Big[V_{19}-V_0+\frac{R_cT_{19}}{V_{19}}\Big(1-\frac{P_0}{P_{19}}\Big)\Big]\Big\},\qquad S=\frac{f}{(1+\alpha)F/\dot m_0}$$
$$\eta_o = \frac{(1+\alpha)(F/\dot m_0)V_0}{f\,h_{PR}},\qquad \eta_{th} = \frac{(1+f)\tilde V_9^2 + \alpha\tilde V_{19}^2 - (1+\alpha)V_0^2}{2 f h_{PR}},\qquad \eta_p = \eta_o/\eta_{th}$$

Here \(\tilde V\) is the velocity the jet would reach if it kept expanding isentropically from the nozzle-exit static state down to \(P_0\). With a choked convergent nozzle, energy held as exit over-pressure then still counts as thermal output. The cost of not expanding it fully shows up as a lower \(\eta_p\), which keeps \(\eta_p\) below 1. For ideally expanded nozzles, \(\tilde V = V\).

2.3 Off-design matching

For off-design I use Mattingly's performance method [1]: the HPT nozzle (4) and LPT nozzle (4.5, after coolant mixing) throats are choked and fixed in area, and the component adiabatic efficiencies (evaluated with the real-gas \(h\) and \(s^\circ\)) and the cooling fraction stay at their design values. For a constant-cp gas without cooling, two choked throats in series fix the HPT operating point. With variable properties and coolant mixed between the throats, it moves slightly: the HPT exit temperature is solved so that the 4.5 throat passes the HPT flow plus the coolant. Choked and unchoked mass flux \(\rho V\) follow from \(h\) and \(s^\circ\) as in the nozzles. Continuity through the core and fan nozzles (convergent, choked or unchoked) gives the LPT exit state and the bypass ratio:

$$\dot m_4 = A_4\,(\rho V)^*(T_{t4},P_{t4}),\qquad A_{4.5}(\rho V)^*(T_{t4.5},P_{t4.5}) = A_8\,(\rho V)(T_{t5},\pi_{tL}\pi_nP_{t4.5},P_0) = (1+f)\dot m_c,\qquad \alpha = \frac{A_{18}(\rho V)(T_{t13},\pi_{fn}P_{t13},P_0)}{\dot m_c}$$

The LPC work follows the fan work on the shared shaft, \(\Delta h_{cL} = K\Delta h_f\), with \(K\) frozen at design (Mattingly's \(\tau_{cL}=\tau_f\) is the constant-cp case \(K=1\)). With throttle set by \(T_{t4}\), the only outer unknown is the fan work, found by a Brent root solve of the LP-spool power balance. Inside each evaluation, the HP spool, burner and HPT are converged by fixed-point iteration on \(T_{t3}\) with a Brent solve for the HPT exit temperature, and the LPT exit temperature comes from a second Brent solve. Absolute size enters only through the design mass flow, so thrust lapse and TSFC do not depend on it.

2.4 Fan size and installation penalty

The fan diameter comes from the total air flow passing the fan face at an axial Mach number \(M_2 = 0.60\) through an annulus with hub/tip ratio 0.30. The installation penalty has two parts. Nacelle skin-friction drag is \(q_0 C_f FQ\,S_{wet}\), with \(C_f\) from the Schlichting turbulent flat-plate law [7], form × interference factor \(FQ=1.4\), and a nacelle 1.2 fan diameters across and 1.5 long. Weight drag is \(m g/(L/D)\), with \(L/D = 17\) and propulsion-system mass taken proportional to \(D^2\), anchored at 2,400 kg for a 1.55 m fan (roughly a CFM56-7B-class dry mass). Because both terms scale with \(D^2\propto\dot m_0\), the penalty as a fraction of thrust is

$$\phi = \frac{D_{nac}+D_W}{F} = \frac{c_{pen}}{F/\dot m_0},\qquad S_{inst} = \frac{S}{1-\phi},$$

where \(c_{pen}\) depends only on flight condition and the assumptions above. At M 0.78 / 35,000 ft it is m/s ( nacelle drag + weight). Every assumption in \(c_{pen}\) is crude, so I report results with \(c_{pen}\) halved and doubled as well.

2.5 Implementation

The Python package (tfcycle) has modules for the atmosphere (1976 US Standard, all layers to 86 km [3]), the gas model, on-design, off-design and installation. run_study.py regenerates every number on this page into data/*.json in about 40 s. The in-browser tool (§6) runs code/js/tfcycle.js, a line-by-line JavaScript port with the same equations and the same Brent algorithm. 3.1 Ideal-cycle limit

With the constant-cp fallback (thermo='cpg'), no cooling air, polytropic, burner, mechanical and pressure-ratio efficiencies all set to 1, one gas (\(\gamma=1.4\), \(c_p=1004\) J/kg·K), ideally expanded nozzles and fuel mass neglected in the flow terms, the real-cycle code must reduce to Mattingly's ideal turbofan:

$$\frac{F}{\dot m_0} = \frac{a_0}{1+\alpha}\Big[\frac{V_9}{a_0}-M_0+\alpha\Big(\frac{V_{19}}{a_0}-M_0\Big)\Big],\quad \frac{V_9}{a_0}=\sqrt{\tfrac{2}{\gamma-1}\Big[\tau_\lambda-\tau_r\big(\tau_c-1+\alpha(\tau_f-1)\big)-\tfrac{\tau_\lambda}{\tau_r\tau_c}\Big]},\quad \frac{V_{19}}{a_0}=\sqrt{\tfrac{2}{\gamma-1}(\tau_r\tau_f-1)}$$

3.3 Optimum fan pressure ratio

For the ideal cycle, TSFC at fixed \(\alpha\) is minimised when the two jets leave at the same speed, \(V_{19}=V_9\), which gives

$$\tau_f^{*} = \frac{\tau_\lambda-\tau_r(\tau_c-1)-\tau_\lambda/(\tau_r\tau_c)+\alpha\tau_r+1}{\tau_r(1+\alpha)}.$$

A golden-section search on the full code lands on \(\pi_f^*\) to within (a flat minimum limits this to roughly the square root of machine precision), with \(V_{19}/V_9 = 1\) at the optimum. For the lossy cycle, Mattingly gives the approximate condition \(V_{19}/V_9\approx\eta_f\eta_{tL}\). Working through the marginal energy exchange with polytropic components gives the exact first-order condition (ideally expanded nozzles):

$$\frac{V_{19}}{V_9}\bigg|_{opt} = \eta_{mL}\,\frac{1-x_{19}(1-e_f)}{1+x_9\,(1/e_{tL}-1)},\qquad x_{19}=\frac{T_{19}}{T_{t19}},\ \ x_9=\frac{T_9}{T_{t9}}.$$

The derivation holds for variable \(c_p\), because \(dh(T_s)=(T_s/T_t)(1-e)\,dw\) follows from the entropy-function form of the polytropic relation. Shift work \(dw\) per unit bypass air from core to bypass stream. The bypass jet gains kinetic energy \(dw[1-x_{19}(1-e_f)]\), because the extra pressure ratio also lets the existing flow expand further. The core jet loses \((dw/\eta_{mL})[1+x_9(1/e_{tL}-1)]\), the turbine reheat penalty. Fuel flow does not change. Setting \(dF=0\) gives the ratio.

The numerical optimum satisfies this exact condition to . Mattingly's simpler form is off by over BPR 4–12. That is expected for an approximation, and it tends to put the optimum fan pressure ratio a little low.

5.5 Installation: why fan diameter limits BPR

Without installation effects, TSFC keeps falling to an optimum near BPR (\(\pi_f\) ≈ ). Beyond that, the fixed fan and nozzle pressure losses consume the small fan pressure rise. Charging nacelle drag and weight to the engine moves the optimum to BPR (\(\pi_f\) ≈ , φ = of gross thrust). The optimum is flat: installed TSFC is within 0.5 % of its minimum from BPR . 7Limitations & future work

  • Simple cooling, no bleed or power extraction. One coolant stream (a fixed of core air) bypasses the burner and HPT and is remixed losslessly ahead of the LPT. A fuller model would split nozzle-guide-vane and rotor cooling (the NGV coolant does HPT work) and add mixing pressure losses, customer bleed and shaft offtake.
  • Thermodynamics. Complete lean combustion without dissociation (adequate below about 2000 K), a CH1.92 fuel surrogate, the lower heating value referred to 298.15 K, and fuel entering at 298.15 K.
  • No component maps. Off-design uses constant adiabatic efficiencies and choked turbine nozzles, with no surge-margin, fan-speed or corrected-flow limits beyond the Tt4 cap. That is adequate for lapse trends, not for detailed ratings.
  • Installation model. The penalty is skin friction plus weight scaling with \(D^2\). It has no spillage, no core-cowl scrubbing drag, no gearbox or LPT stage-count weights and no ground-clearance constraint. The optimum is flat, so its location is sensitive to these choices; the penalty sweep is there to show that.
  • Low-BPR engine. The F100-class case is treated as separate-flow and dry. The real engine is mixed-flow with an afterburner.
  • Public inputs. Fan pressure ratios, pressure-ratio splits and efficiencies are my plausible assumptions, not manufacturer data.
