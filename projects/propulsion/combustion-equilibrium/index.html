A from-scratch chemical-equilibrium solver for liquid bipropellants: why methalox, kerolox and hydrolox engines run at the mixture ratios they do

Abstract

I wrote a small CEA-style chemical-equilibrium code for the C–H–O system: Gibbs free-energy minimisation with the element-potential Newton iteration of Gordon & McBride (NASA RP-1311), NASA 7-coefficient thermodynamic data for 14 gas species, adiabatic (HP) combustion, and isentropic (SP) nozzle expansion with frozen or shifting composition. The code conserves elements to relative error, reproduces a calorically perfect nozzle to , and gives stoichiometric flame temperatures of K (H2–O2) and K (CH4–air) against textbook values of ≈3080 K and ≈2226 K. Sweeps at 10 MPa show vacuum-Isp peaks at O/F ≈ (LOX/LH2), (LOX/CH4) and (LOX/RP-1), all fuel-rich of stoichiometric because a lighter exhaust beats a hotter one. Real engines sit off these peaks for reasons outside the Isp curve: tank volume and density impulse push hydrogen engines towards O/F ≈ 6, while turbine and wall temperatures keep kerosene engines on the fuel-rich side. For four representative engines, the publicly quoted delivered vacuum Isp is – % of the shifting-equilibrium ideal, and lies close to the frozen-flow value.

1Motivation

A liquid rocket engine's mixture ratio O/F (oxidiser mass flow over fuel mass flow) is one of the first numbers fixed in a design, and it is rarely the stoichiometric value or the one that maximises chamber temperature. The ideal-rocket result \(c^* \propto \sqrt{T_c/\mathcal{M}}\) already shows why: running fuel-rich lowers the chamber temperature a little but lowers the exhaust molar mass \(\mathcal{M}\) a lot, especially when the excess fuel is hydrogen. Putting numbers on this needs the equilibrium composition of a 3500–3900 K gas that is partly dissociated into H, O and OH, which is what codes such as NASA CEA compute.

The purpose of this study is to build that capability from first principles, verify it carefully, and then use it to answer three concrete questions: where the theoretical Isp peaks for each propellant pair; how much chamber pressure matters through dissociation; and why real engines (Merlin, RS-25, Raptor, RD-180 classes) operate where they do, including how far their delivered Isp falls short of the ideal number.

2Method

Equilibrium by Gibbs minimisation

For an ideal-gas mixture of species \(j\) with \(n_j\) moles per kilogram of propellant, equilibrium at fixed \(T,P\) is the minimum of

$$G=\sum_j n_j\mu_j,\qquad \frac{\mu_j}{RT}=\frac{h_j^\circ(T)}{RT}-\frac{s_j^\circ(T)}{R}+\ln\frac{n_j}{n}+\ln\frac{P}{P^\circ}$$

subject to element conservation \(\sum_j a_{ij}n_j=b_i^\circ\). Introducing element potentials \(\pi_i\) (scaled Lagrange multipliers), stationarity gives \(\mu_j/RT=\sum_i a_{ij}\pi_i\). Following RP-1311 [1], the Newton corrections for every species are written in terms of a few scalar unknowns,

$$\Delta\ln n_j=-\frac{\mu_j}{RT}+\sum_i a_{ij}\pi_i+\Delta\ln n+\frac{H_j^\circ}{RT}\,\Delta\ln T,$$

which, substituted into the linearised element balances, the total-mole identity and (for HP problems) the energy equation, leaves a symmetric system of size (elements + 2) whatever the number of species. For C–H–O that is a 5×5 solve per iteration. The element rows and the enthalpy row are

$$\sum_i\Big(\sum_j a_{kj}a_{ij}n_j\Big)\pi_i+\Big(\sum_j a_{kj}n_j\Big)\Delta\ln n+\Big(\sum_j a_{kj}n_j\tfrac{H_j^\circ}{RT}\Big)\Delta\ln T=b_k^\circ-b_k+\sum_j a_{kj}n_j\tfrac{\mu_j}{RT}$$
$$\sum_i\Big(\sum_j a_{ij}n_j\tfrac{H_j^\circ}{RT}\Big)\pi_i+\Big(\sum_j n_j\tfrac{H_j^\circ}{RT}\Big)\Delta\ln n+\Big(\sum_j n_j\tfrac{c_{p,j}^\circ}{R}+\sum_j n_j\tfrac{(H_j^\circ)^2}{(RT)^2}\Big)\Delta\ln T=\frac{h_0-h}{RT}+\sum_j n_j\tfrac{H_j^\circ}{RT}\tfrac{\mu_j}{RT}$$

For SP (isentropic) problems the enthalpy row is replaced by the corresponding entropy row. Steps are damped as in RP-1311 §3.3: \(\lambda=\min(1,\lambda_1,\lambda_2)\) with \(\lambda_1=2/\max(5|\Delta\ln T|,5|\Delta\ln n|,|\Delta\ln n_j|)\) over the major species, and \(\lambda_2\) stopping a trace species from jumping past a mole fraction of ≈10−4 in one step. Iteration stops when the weighted corrections fall below 10−11 with a full Newton step, several orders tighter than CEA's default, so that conservation errors are at round-off level.

Equilibrium derivatives \((\partial\ln V/\partial\ln T)_P\) and \((\partial\ln V/\partial\ln P)_T\) come from the same matrix with two new right-hand sides, giving the equilibrium \(c_p\), and

$$c_v=c_p+\frac{nR\,(\partial\ln V/\partial\ln T)_P^2}{(\partial\ln V/\partial\ln P)_T},\qquad \gamma_s=-\frac{c_p/c_v}{(\partial\ln V/\partial\ln P)_T},\qquad a^2=nRT\gamma_s .$$

Thermodynamic data

Each species uses NASA 7-coefficient polynomials in two ranges joined at 1000 K. I typed the coefficients in from the GRI-Mech 3.0 thermo file [3] (itself compiled from Burcat/TPIS/JANAF), with one substitution: OH uses the later Burcat fit [4], because the GRI fit carries \(\Delta h_f^\circ\approx+39\) kJ/mol against the currently accepted ≈+37.3 kJ/mol. The set is H2, O2, H, O, OH, H2O, HO2, H2O2, CO, CO2, CH4, CH3, HCO, CH2O, plus N2, NO and Ar for the air and exact-solution checks. Because the coefficients were typed by hand, every species is tested (Section 3) for its 298 K heat of formation and entropy and for continuity at 1000 K. Condensed carbon is not included: at the lowest O/F studied the oxygen-to-carbon atom ratio is still (RP-1) and (CH4), so all carbon can leave as CO and soot is not an equilibrium product in the chamber.

Rocket performance

The combustor is treated as infinite-area (no Rayleigh loss). The chamber state is an HP equilibrium at \(P_c\) with the reactant enthalpy \(h_0\). Expansion is isentropic, \(s=s_c\), and the velocity is \(u=\sqrt{2(h_0-h)}\). The throat is the pressure at which \(u=a\) (Newton on \(\ln P_c/P\)); the exit is the supersonic pressure giving the area ratio \(\varepsilon=\rho_t u_t/(\rho_e u_e)\), found by a safeguarded Newton iteration with slope \(d\ln\varepsilon/d\ln(P_c/P)=(1-M^{-2})/\gamma\). Then

$$c^*=\frac{P_c}{\rho_t u_t},\qquad I_{\rm vac}=\frac{u_e}{g_0}+\frac{P_e\varepsilon}{g_0\rho_t u_t},\qquad I_{\rm SL}=I_{\rm vac}-\frac{P_a\varepsilon}{g_0\rho_t u_t},\qquad C_F=\frac{g_0 I}{c^*}.$$

Shifting expansion re-equilibrates at every pressure; frozen expansion keeps the chamber composition. These bracket the real, kinetically limited nozzle. Density impulse uses the bulk density \(\rho_b=(1+r)/(r/\rho_o+1/\rho_f)\) with \(r=\) O/F: \(I_d=\rho_b I_{\rm sp}\), reported in s·g/cm³.

The Python package (code/eqsolver) is the reference implementation; js/eqsolver.js is a line-by-line port used by the interactive tool, reading the same coefficient table from data/thermo.json.

Three further checks establish that the answer is the minimum of \(G\), not just a stationary point of the algorithm:

  • Perturbation. At the LOX/CH4 chamber state, 200 random element-preserving perturbations of the composition all raise \(G/RT\) (smallest increase ), and halving the perturbation divides the increase by \(2^{p}\) with median \(p=\), the quadratic behaviour of a smooth minimum.
  • Independent optimiser. Minimising \(G\) directly with SciPy's SLSQP (a general constrained optimiser, not RP-1311) gives major-species mole fractions within relative of this code, and no lower \(G\).
  • Newton convergence. Figure 1 shows the convergence of the HP solve for LOX/LH2. After the damped start the error falls quadratically, e.g. → → . Chamber states converge in about 9–10 iterations from a cold start.

Exact solution for the nozzle

A monatomic gas has exactly \(c_p=\tfrac52R\) in the NASA fit, so a "rocket" burning hot argon must reproduce the closed-form ideal-rocket relations with \(\gamma=5/3\). With \(T_c=3000\) K, \(P_c=5\) MPa and \(\varepsilon=10\), the solver matches the exact \(c^*\), \(P_e/P_c\) and \(C_F\) to relative (both frozen and shifting paths). This validates the throat and area-ratio iterations independently of the chemistry.

5Results & discussion

5.1 Isp against mixture ratio

Every pair peaks fuel-rich of stoichiometric. The shifting vacuum optimum is at O/F for hydrogen ( s), for methane ( s) and for RP-1 ( s). The frozen optimum is further fuel-rich still (, , ): near stoichiometric much of the chemical energy is held in dissociated H, O and OH, which a frozen nozzle never gets back. The hydrogen curve is very flat, losing only a few seconds between O/F 4 and 6, so the Isp penalty of running at O/F 6 is small.

The gap between shifting and frozen is largest for methane at this condition ( % at O/F 3.6) and smallest for hydrogen at O/F 6 ( %). It measures how much recombination energy is available, and it bounds what finite-rate chemistry can do in a real nozzle.

5.2 Chamber temperature and molar mass

\(T_c\) peaks at O/F ( K) for hydrogen, ( K) for methane and ( K) for RP-1, slightly fuel-rich of stoichiometric because dissociation caps the temperature. The Isp peaks are well to the left of these points. Moving fuel-rich from the \(T_c\) peak loses little temperature at first, but the molar mass falls steeply, most of all for hydrogen, where the extra H2 (2 g/mol) replaces H2O (18 g/mol). Since \(c^*\propto\sqrt{T_c/\mathcal{M}}\), the optimum sits where the fractional drop in \(T_c\) starts to outpace the fractional drop in \(\mathcal{M}\).

5.3 Exhaust composition

On the fuel-rich side the chamber gas is H2O and H2 (plus CO and CO2 for the hydrocarbons), with a few per cent of H and OH. Towards and past stoichiometric, OH, O and O2 rise by orders of magnitude, which is where frozen and shifting results diverge most. CH4, HCO and CH2O stay at trace level throughout. Including them costs nothing and confirms that no hydrocarbon survives in the chamber at these O/F.

5.4 Chamber pressure and dissociation

Raising \(P_c\) from 0.5 to 30 MPa suppresses dissociation (Le Chatelier: dissociation increases the mole number) and raises \(T_c\) by K for LOX/LH2 and K for LOX/RP-1. At a fixed area ratio, shifting vacuum Isp rises only modestly ( s for LH2) because a shifting nozzle recovers most of the dissociation energy anyway. Frozen Isp rises much more ( s), because at high pressure less energy is locked in radicals to begin with. The practical value of high chamber pressure is therefore less the ideal Isp at fixed ε than the smaller, lighter engine for a given thrust and the larger area ratio it permits at sea level. The chemical benefit is that real, partly frozen nozzles lose less.

5.5 Density impulse: why hydrogen engines run at O/F ≈ 6

Liquid hydrogen has a density of about 71 kg/m³, so at the Isp optimum most of the tank volume holds fuel. Raising O/F from the Isp optimum () to 6 costs s of vacuum Isp but raises the bulk propellant density by %. In Figure 6 hydrogen's density impulse is still climbing at O/F 10. The chosen O/F ≈ 6 of RS-25-class and upper-stage hydrolox engines is a vehicle-level compromise between Isp and tank mass and drag. For methane, density impulse peaks at O/F ≈ , fuel-lean of the Isp peak, and Raptor-class engines run near 3.6, between the two. For RP-1 the density impulse peaks at , yet Merlin-class engines run at ≈2.36, fuel-rich of both peaks. The reasons are not in the Isp curve: a fuel-rich gas generator or preburner needs turbine-inlet temperatures far below \(T_c\), a cooler and more reducing gas protects the chamber wall and film-cooled throat, and and at O/F 2.36 the chamber runs about K cooler than at the \(T_c\) peak (Figure 3) for a small Isp penalty. The RD-180 class, at ≈2.72, sits close to the Isp peak. Its oxygen-rich preburner means the turbine limit does not push the main chamber fuel-rich in the same way.

An overall efficiency \(\eta_{I}=\eta_{c^*}\,\eta_{C_F}\) combines combustion efficiency (mixing, incomplete reaction; \(\eta_{c^*}\) typically ≈0.97–0.99 for well-developed engines [6]) with nozzle losses: divergence, boundary-layer drag, and chemical non-equilibrium in the expansion, which on its own moves the result from shifting towards frozen. I cannot split \(\eta_I\) into those factors without measured chamber pressure and flow rates, so the ratios in Table 6 are overall figures.

The RS-25-class engine shows the highest ratio ( %): a large, bell-contoured, staged-combustion hydrogen engine, where low exhaust molar mass and high pressure keep the frozen–shifting gap small. The Merlin-class figure ( %) is lowest, and below even the frozen ideal, as expected for an open gas-generator cycle: a few per cent of the propellant drives the turbine and is dumped at low Isp, which the quoted engine Isp includes. The sea-level ratios also absorb over-expansion effects, and the ideal model does not include flow separation.

7Limitations & future work

  • Thermo data range. The GRI-Mech fits are nominally valid to 3500 K. The Raptor- and RD-180-class chambers (≈3760 K and ≈3890 K) extrapolate the high-range polynomials by a few hundred kelvin. The fits are smooth there, but a CEA run with its 200–6000 K data would be the right cross-check. Replacing the table with NASA Glenn 9-coefficient data [8] is the obvious next step.
  • Species set. 14 C–H–O gases. No condensed phases (soot, ice) and no ions. Graphite could in principle become stable at the cold end of a shifting expansion at the most fuel-rich RP-1 points; I did not test that.
  • RP-1 surrogate. A single formula with an approximate heat of formation. Real RP-1 varies by batch, and small changes in \(\Delta h_f\) move \(T_c\) by tens of kelvin.
  • Ideal nozzle. Infinite-area combustor, 1-D isentropic flow, no divergence, boundary-layer or kinetic losses, and no flow separation at sea level. A finite-area combustor and a one-dimensional finite-rate kinetics (Bray-type freezing-point) model would sit between the frozen and shifting curves.
  • Engine inputs. Public, rounded figures. The delivered-Isp comparisons use publicly quoted numbers that differ between sources and engine versions, so the efficiencies are good to perhaps ±1 %.
