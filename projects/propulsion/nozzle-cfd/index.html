Quasi-1D Euler CFD of rocket nozzles: shock position, over-expansion and separation risk along an ascent trajectory

Dulran · September 2026

Finite volume · HLLC · MUSCL SSP-RK3 Exact normal-shock-in-nozzle solution US Standard Atmosphere 1976 Summerfield criterion Interactive solver ↓

Abstract

I wrote a quasi-one-dimensional Euler solver for convergent-divergent nozzles (cell-centred finite volumes, HLLC Riemann solver, van Albada-limited MUSCL reconstruction, third-order SSP Runge-Kutta, reservoir inflow and a back-pressure outflow that switches between subsonic and supersonic exits) and an exact solver for the same problem (isentropic area-Mach relation plus the normal shock in the divergent section). Against the exact solution the scheme converges at an observed order of in the L1 Mach error, conserves mass to round-off, and places a captured normal shock within of a cell of the exact position. I then applied it to representative Merlin 1D-, RS-25- and Raptor-class sea-level nozzles (chamber pressure and expansion ratio from public sources, exhaust γ = 1.2) along a 0–50 km ascent. The inviscid normal shock would need a back pressure at least times sea-level ambient to reach the exit plane of any of the three, so inviscid theory alone never predicts a shock inside these nozzles. The practical limit is flow separation, which a quasi-1D inviscid model cannot compute. I flag it with the empirical Summerfield criterion (pe/pa < 0.4). The Merlin- and Raptor-class nozzles sit on the safe side at lift-off. The RS-25-class nozzle, at pe/pa ≈ , does not, even though it has more than twice the Merlin's chamber pressure.

1Motivation

A first-stage engine fires from sea level into near-vacuum. A larger expansion ratio ε = Ae/A* gives more thrust at altitude. At lift-off, though, the exit pressure pe of a large nozzle falls well below ambient. An over-expanded nozzle loses pressure thrust, and if it is far enough over-expanded the boundary layer separates from the wall. The asymmetric separation line that results produces side loads, which have damaged nozzles and test stands. Designers therefore trade ε against chamber pressure pc: for a fixed ε, a higher pc raises pe in proportion.

This study builds the simplest CFD tool that can say something quantitative about that trade: a quasi-1D Euler solver, verified properly, plus a standard atmosphere. For each engine I ask four questions. How far over-expanded is the nozzle along the ascent? Where would an inviscid normal shock have to stand? At what altitude does pe = pa? And how much margin in ε or pc does each design have against a common empirical separation criterion?

2Method

Governing equations

For a duct of slowly varying area A(x), the quasi-1D Euler equations in conservation form are

$$\frac{\partial (\mathbf{Q}A)}{\partial t} + \frac{\partial (\mathbf{F}A)}{\partial x} = \mathbf{S},\qquad \mathbf{Q}=\begin{pmatrix}\rho\\ \rho u\\ E\end{pmatrix},\; \mathbf{F}=\begin{pmatrix}\rho u\\ \rho u^2+p\\ (E+p)u\end{pmatrix},\; \mathbf{S}=\begin{pmatrix}0\\ p\,\dfrac{dA}{dx}\\ 0\end{pmatrix},$$

with \(E = p/(\gamma-1) + \tfrac12\rho u^2\) for a calorically perfect gas. I set γ = 1.2 for the rocket cases, a typical frozen value for hydrocarbon or hydrogen combustion products, and γ = 1.4 for the air benchmarks. Everything is non-dimensionalised by the reservoir state (p0 = ρ0 = 1, so RT0 = 1). The solution therefore depends only on γ, the area law and pb/p0. R and T0 only rescale dimensional velocities and mass flow, and they drop out of the thrust coefficient.

Finite-volume discretisation

  • Cells. N uniform cells with volume \(V_i = A(x_i)\,\Delta x\) and face areas \(A_{i\pm1/2}\). The update is
    $$V_i\frac{d\mathbf{Q}_i}{dt} = -\big(\hat{\mathbf{F}}_{i+1/2}A_{i+1/2} - \hat{\mathbf{F}}_{i-1/2}A_{i-1/2}\big) + \begin{pmatrix}0\\ p_i\,(A_{i+1/2}-A_{i-1/2})\\ 0\end{pmatrix}.$$
    Writing the pressure source with the same face areas as the flux makes a gas at rest an exact discrete steady state in any duct. A unit test checks this to 10−13.
  • Riemann solver. HLLC (Toro, Spruce & Speares) with wave-speed bounds \(S_L = \min(u_L-c_L,\,u_R-c_R,\,\tilde u-\tilde c)\) and the mirror-image \(S_R\), where tildes are Roe averages. The Roe-average (Einfeldt) bounds let the transonic rarefaction at the throat through without an explicit entropy fix. Adding the Davis bounds made the solver robust when a strong back pressure first meets supersonic exit flow.
  • Reconstruction. MUSCL on the primitive variables (ρ, u, p) with the van Albada limiter. I use it rather than minmod or van Leer because it is smooth: with minmod and van Leer the residual stalled in limit cycles on the shock cases, while van Albada converges to 10−11. There is a first-order fallback at any face where the reconstructed ρ or p would be non-positive.
  • Time marching. Three-stage, third-order strong-stability-preserving Runge-Kutta (Shu & Osher) at CFL 0.8. To reach the steady state faster I use local time stepping (Δti = CFL·Δx/(|u|+c)i). The browser tool can also run a global, time-accurate step so the start-up transient looks physical. Iteration stops when the RMS density residual has fallen by 11 orders of magnitude.
  • Initial condition. Reservoir gas at rest fills the nozzle, and the back pressure is applied at the exit at t = 0, which is the physical start-up. A start from a supersonic Mach ramp can leave a shock that belongs just inside the exit stuck on the outflow boundary as a spurious discrete steady state. Starting from rest avoids this.

Boundary conditions

  • Inflow (subsonic reservoir). Two ghost cells take the velocity extrapolated from the interior (minmod-limited linear extrapolation, clipped below Mach 0.95). Static T, p and ρ then follow from the prescribed p0, T0 through \(c_pT + u^2/2 = c_pT_0\) and isentropic relations.
  • Outflow. The exit-face state is extrapolated from the interior. If it is supersonic and pb is below the pressure behind a normal shock at that Mach number, every variable is extrapolated, because nothing from outside can enter. Otherwise ρ and u are extrapolated and p = pb is imposed, so a subsonic exit sees the back pressure and a strong back pressure drives a shock into the nozzle. A last check handles a captured shock that sits in the final cells when pb is too low for any steady shock there: the exit is then treated as supersonic from the upstream state, which lets a start-up shock leave the nozzle.

Exact reference solution

For choked flow, the isentropic branch is the area-Mach relation

$$\frac{A}{A^*}=\frac{1}{M}\left[\frac{2}{\gamma+1}\left(1+\frac{\gamma-1}{2}M^2\right)\right]^{\frac{\gamma+1}{2(\gamma-1)}},$$

which I invert on the subsonic or supersonic branch with a bracketed root solve. For a back pressure between the "shock at exit" value pNSE and the "subsonic isentropic" value psub, the exit Mach number follows in closed form from \(p_eA_e/(p_{01}A^*)\). That fixes the stagnation-pressure ratio \(p_{02}/p_{01}\), which is inverted for the upstream shock Mach number M1, and hence for the shock area ratio As/A* (Anderson, Modern Compressible Flow, ch. 5). Downstream of the shock the flow is subsonic-isentropic with \(A_2^* = A^*\,p_{01}/p_{02}\). The same module gives the critical back pressures psub, pNSE and psup, and the inverse map from shock station to back pressure.

Performance and separation flags

The thrust coefficient is taken from the CFD exit-face flux, not from the isentropic formula:

$$C_F=\frac{\dot m\,u_e + (p_e-p_a)A_e}{p_c A^*} = \underbrace{\frac{(\rho u^2+p)_eA_e}{p_cA^*}}_{C_{F,\mathrm{vac}}\;\text{(CFD face flux)}} - \frac{p_a}{p_c}\,\varepsilon .$$

While the exit is supersonic the internal flow cannot feel pa, so a single converged solution per engine gives CF(h) for every altitude. I checked this by re-running each engine at the 10 km and 30 km ambient pressures (section 5). Ambient pressure comes from the 1976 US Standard Atmosphere, using its first five layers (troposphere, tropopause, two stratospheric lapse layers and the 47–51 km isothermal layer) with the geometric-to-geopotential altitude conversion.

Separation is not computed. The model is inviscid and one-dimensional, so the flow in it cannot separate. Instead I flag separation risk with the Summerfield criterion, pe/pa < 0.4. This is an empirical rule of thumb from 1950s conical-nozzle tests, and values from about 0.25 to 0.4 are quoted in the literature, so 0.4 is the conservative end. For comparison I also evaluate Schmucker's correlation, \(p_{\mathrm{sep}}/p_a = (1.88\,M_e - 1)^{-0.64}\), which falls with exit Mach number. Other criteria exist (e.g. Kalt–Badal, Stark). Real separated flow, whether free-shock (FSS) or restricted-shock (RSS), is two-dimensional, viscous and often unsteady.

4Case studies

The three engines span the lift-off design space for current sea-level boosters. The inputs are representative values from public sources, and they are approximate. The exhaust is modelled as a calorically perfect gas with γ = 1.2 for all three, which ignores the real differences in mixture composition and shifting equilibrium between LOX/RP-1, LOX/LH2 and LOX/CH4. Section 5 gives a γ sensitivity check. In quasi-1D inviscid flow the exit state depends only on ε and γ, not on the contour, so each engine uses the same smooth bell-like area law (a parabolic convergent section with contraction ratio 4 and a cubic divergent section with zero slope at throat and exit) scaled to its ε, on N = cells.

5.3 Where would the normal shock stand?

Textbook quasi-1D theory puts a normal shock inside an over-expanded nozzle once pb > pNSE. For these engines that threshold is far above any real ambient pressure.

The quasi-1D normal shock is therefore the wrong model for real over-expansion. In a real nozzle the adverse pressure gradient of an oblique shock system separates the boundary layer long before a normal shock could stand in the core. What the inviscid solution does provide is the attached-flow exit state that the empirical criteria take as input, and it does that accurately.

5.4 Design space: how much ε can a given pc carry at sea level?

For fixed γ and a supersonic exit, pe/pc depends only on ε. The Summerfield limit pe ≥ 0.4 pa therefore gives a maximum sea-level-safe expansion ratio for each chamber pressure, and equivalently a minimum pc for each ε. That minimum is also the lowest throttle setting at which the nozzle stays clear of the flag at lift-off.

Why the RS-25 can have ε = 69 at all. For a fixed ε, pe scales with pc. The same ε = 69 nozzle fed at the Merlin-class 9.7 MPa would give pe/pa ≈ at lift-off, deep in the separated regime by any criterion. The RS-25's roughly doubled chamber pressure is what brings it to the edge of the envelope. Even so, in this model it sits beyond the Summerfield line: at its pc the criterion allows ε ≈ , and meeting it at ε = 69 would take pc ≈ MPa. The real engine has a long record of sea-level operation, so I read this as a limit of the criteria and of the model, not as a prediction of separation. There are three reasons. The 0.4 threshold comes from low-Mach conical nozzles, and the exit-Mach-dependent Schmucker limit is already less restrictive. The quasi-1D exit pressure is a one-dimensional average of a contoured bell's wall pressure. And the frozen-γ exit pressure is sensitive to γ (Table 9). The robust conclusion is comparative: the RS-25-class design has by far the least sea-level margin of the three and relies on high pc to be viable. The Merlin- and Raptor-class nozzles could stay above the line down to about % and % of their nominal pc, respectively. Start-up and shut-down, when pc passes through low values, are therefore when all three are exposed. That matches the well-documented concern with transient side loads in large sea-level nozzles.

7Limitations & future work

  • No separation physics. The model is inviscid and quasi-1D, so it cannot represent boundary-layer separation, free- vs restricted-shock separation, or side loads. Separation appears here only as an empirical flag, and the Summerfield threshold carries large scatter (≈ 0.25–0.4 in the literature). Resolving it needs at least axisymmetric RANS with a validated turbulence model.
  • Quasi-1D flow. Real bell nozzles have radial pressure gradients and internal shocks from the contour. The wall pressure that matters for separation is not the 1-D mean. Divergence losses (≈ 1–2 % in CF for a bell) are not included.
  • Frozen, calorically perfect exhaust. One γ for the whole expansion, with no shifting equilibrium, condensation or two-phase effects. Table 9 shows the RS-25-class conclusion is sensitive to this.
  • Inputs. The engine parameters are approximate public values, and the Raptor figures are uncertain. No combustion-chamber losses (Rayleigh heating, c* efficiency) or film cooling are modelled, and ambient pa is the static pressure of a standard day with no plume–freestream interaction or base flow.
  • Numerics. The shock position is first-order, as for any capturing scheme. Within about 1 % of pNSE the choice between "shock just inside the exit" and "supersonic exit" is only resolved to discretisation accuracy.
  • Next steps: an axisymmetric Euler/RANS solver on a method-of-characteristics bell contour, a shifting-equilibrium exhaust from a chemical-equilibrium code, and a check of the separation flag against published cold-flow separation data.
